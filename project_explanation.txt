1. WHAT IS THIS PROJECT? (THE BIG PICTURE IN SIMPLE WORDS)
================================================================================
Imagine you run a platform like YouTube, Netflix, or Amazon. 
You have:
- Millions of items (videos, movies, or products).
- Millions of users who open the app every second.
- Only a tiny screen space to show 5 to 10 recommendations.

You face two big engineering challenges:

CHALLENGE 1: The Recommendation Dilemma (Speed vs. Accuracy)
  If you try to run a heavy, super-smart machine learning model on all 10 million 
  items for every single user, the app will freeze or take seconds to respond. 
  Users will leave.
  -> SOLUTION: Build a "Two-Stage Recommender":
     * Stage 1 (Fast Net): Filter 10,000,000 items down to ~100 candidate items in 
       under 5 milliseconds using vector search (Faiss).
     * Stage 2 (Smart Judge): Score only those 100 items using a sophisticated 
       gradient-boosted ranking model (LightGBM) to pick the ultimate top 10.

CHALLENGE 2: The Decision Dilemma (Did our new AI actually make things better?)
  How do tech companies prove that their new recommender actually increased watch 
  time or sales, rather than users just getting lucky or seasonal spikes?
  -> SOLUTION: Run an end-to-end A/B test simulation:
     * Send 50% of users to the old system (Control) and 50% to the new system (Treatment).
     * Check for hidden tracking bugs using Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM).
     * Use Netflix's secret mathematical weapon, CUPED, to eliminate user background 
       noise so you can reach confident conclusions twice as fast with fewer users.

2. REAL-WORLD ANALOGY (HOW IT ALL COMES TOGETHER)
================================================================================
Think of this project like a restaurant with 10,000 dishes on the menu:

1. THE LIBRARIAN (Stage 1 - Candidate Retrieval / Faiss):
   You walk into the restaurant. The waiter doesn't read you all 10,000 recipes.
   Instead, in 2 seconds, the librarian looks at your broad preference ("Loves spicy 
   Italian food") and pulls out 50 relevant dishes from the library.

2. THE MASTER CHEF (Stage 2 - Re-Ranking / LightGBM):
   The chef takes those 50 dishes and looks at deeper details: "What did the customer 
   order yesterday? What time of day is it? Are these ingredients fresh right now? 
   How popular is dish #12 this week?"
   The chef scores all 50 dishes and selects the top 5 to present on your special menu.

3. THE SCIENTIFIC EXPERIMENT (A/B Testing with SRM & CUPED):
   The restaurant manager wants to know: Does the new chef's menu make people spend 
   more money than the old menu?
   - The manager splits guests randomly 50/50.
   - The manager uses SRM to ensure no waiters cheated by secretly sending VIPs 
     only to the new menu.
   - The manager uses CUPED to remove the fact that some customers are naturally 
     wealthy spenders, isolating the TRUE effect of the new menu.

3. HOW THE ARCHITECTURE WORKS (STEP-BY-STEP FLOW)
================================================================================

User Arrives at App
       │
       ▼
[STAGE 1: RETRIEVAL]
  - Convert User's taste into a math vector (Embedding).
  - Search 100,000+ items using Faiss in ~3 milliseconds.
  - Output: Top 100 Candidate Items.
       │
       ▼
[STAGE 2: FEATURE ENRICHMENT & RANKING]
  - Combine User Profile (age, past CTR) + Item Profile (popularity, category)
    + Context (recency, match score).
  - Feed these 100 candidates into LightGBM Ranker.
  - Predict exact probability of click / engagement.
  - Output: Top 5 Ranked Recommendations shown to User.
       │
       ▼
[ONLINE EXPERIMENTATION / A/B TESTING]
  - Hash user ID deterministically into Control (A) vs. Treatment (B).
  - Control gets Baseline / Popularity items.
  - Treatment gets Two-Stage Recommender items.
  - Collect user interaction events (Clicks, Purchases).
       │
       ▼
[STATISTICAL VERIFICATION]
  - SRM Check: Did traffic actually split 50/50? (Chi-Square test).
  - CUPED Adjustment: Remove pre-experiment noise to shrink variance.
  - Hypothesis Testing: Compute p-value and determine statistical significance.

5. CORE CONCEPTS & IMPORTANT TERMINOLOGIES EXPLAINED SIMPLY
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART A: RECOMMENDATION SYSTEM TERMINOLOGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. VECTOR EMBEDDING:
   A way of converting complex concepts (like a user's movie taste, or a song's genre) 
   into a list of numbers (a vector) so computers can do math on them.
   * Example: 
     Action movie  = [0.95, 0.10, 0.05]
     Comedy movie  = [0.05, 0.88, 0.12]
     If a user likes action, their vector might be [0.90, 0.15, 0.02].
     Because the user's numbers are close to the action movie's numbers, the computer 
     knows they match!

2. COSINE SIMILARITY / INNER PRODUCT:
   The mathematical formula that measures the angle between two vectors. If two 
   vectors point in the exact same direction, their similarity score is 1.0 (perfect 
   match). If they point opposite, it's -1.0.

3. CANDIDATE GENERATION (RETRIEVAL):
   The first funnel stage. Its only job is HIGH RECALL: don't miss good items, and 
   be blazing fast. It reduces millions of items to a few hundred.

4. RE-RANKING (SCORING):
   The second funnel stage. Its job is HIGH PRECISION: sort the small batch of 
   candidates with maximum accuracy using rich features (like "user clicked this 10 
   minutes ago" or "this item is trending today").

5. APPROXIMATE NEAREST NEIGHBOR (ANN):
   Instead of comparing a user to all 1,000,000 items (which is slow "Exact Search"), 
   ANN algorithms group similar items into clusters or graphs so the search engine 
   only looks in the right neighborhood. It gives you 99% accuracy in 1% of the time.

6. POINTWISE VS. PAIRWISE RANKING (LambdaMART):
   - Pointwise: The model looks at one item at a time and asks: "Will the user click 
     yes (1) or no (0)?"
   - Pairwise (Ranking): The model compares two items at once and asks: "Does the user 
     prefer Item A over Item B?" This mirrors real life much better because human 
     preferences are relative.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PART B: EVALUATION METRICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. RECALL@K:
   "Out of all the items the user actually liked, how many did we manage to include 
   in our top K recommendations?"
   * Example: If the user loved 4 movies, and 3 of them were in your top 20 list, 
     Recall@20 = 3 / 4 = 75%.

2. PRECISION@K:
   "Out of the K recommendations we showed the user, how many did they actually like?"
   * Example: If you showed 10 movies, and the user liked 4 of them, 
     Precision@10 = 4 / 10 = 40%.

3. NDCG@K (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain):
   The most important ranking metric in tech companies!
   Unlike simple precision, NDCG cares about the POSITION of the recommendation.
   - If the user's favorite movie is at Rank #1, you get a huge score.
   - If their favorite movie is buried down at Rank #10, the score is heavily discounted.
   A perfect recommendation list has an NDCG score of 1.0.

4. MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank):
   Measures how quickly the user finds their first relevant item:
   If the first good recommendation is at position 1 -> Score = 1/1 = 1.0.
   If it's at position 2 -> Score = 1/2 = 0.5.
   If it's at position 5 -> Score = 1/5 = 0.2.

PART C: A/B TESTING & EXPERIMENTATION TERMINOLOGIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. CONTROL VS. TREATMENT:
   - Control Group (A): Users who receive the existing/baseline version.
   - Treatment Group (B): Users who receive the new experimental algorithm.

2. DETERMINISTIC TRAFFIC SPLITTING (SALTED HASHING):
   We cannot just flip a coin every time a user opens the app, because a user might 
   be in group A in the morning and group B in the evening (inconsistent experience).
   Instead, we hash their user ID with an experiment salt:
     hash("user_12345" + "exp_recsys_2026") % 100
   This guarantees that User 12345 is always placed in the exact same group, consistently 
   and without storing huge lookup tables in memory.

3. SAMPLE RATIO MISMATCH (SRM):
   Suppose you intended to split traffic 50% Control and 50% Treatment.
   After 1 week, you observe:
     Control: 50,000 users
     Treatment: 45,000 users
   Where did 5,000 treatment users go?
   This is an SRM! It means your experiment is compromised (e.g., your new model 
   crashed on Android phones, or ad-blockers dropped tracking events).
   We use the Chi-Square (χ²) Goodness-of-Fit statistical test to automatically 
   detect SRMs. If an SRM is detected, test results CANNOT be trusted.

4. CUPED (Controlled-experiment using Pre-Experiment Data):
   Invented by researchers at Microsoft and widely used at Netflix, Uber, and Meta.
   - Problem: In any A/B test, users are different. Some people naturally watch 10 hours 
     of video every day; some watch 5 minutes. This huge natural variance creates 
     statistical "noise", making it hard to see if your algorithm caused an improvement.
   - Solution: Use the user's behavior BEFORE the experiment started (covariate X) to 
     subtract out their baseline tendencies from their experiment behavior (metric Y):
       Y_cuped = Y - θ * (X - E[X])
   - Result: The variance of your metric drops dramatically (often by 30% to 50%).
     Lower variance means you can detect smaller improvements much faster with far fewer users!

5. P-VALUE & STATISTICAL SIGNIFICANCE (α):
   The probability that the difference between Treatment and Control was just pure dumb 
   luck. Usually, if p < 0.05 (less than 5% chance of luck), we declare the result 
   "statistically significant" and safe to launch to 100% of users.

6. STATISTICAL POWER (1 - β):
   The probability that your test will successfully detect an improvement if an 
   improvement really exists. Standard industry practice aims for 80% power.

7. MDE (Minimum Detectable Effect):
   The smallest percentage lift (e.g., +1.5% CTR) that your experiment is mathematically 
   capable of proving given your current traffic volume.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
OPTION 3: THE 3 MOST COMMON QUESTIONS & HOW TO ANSWER THEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Question 1: 'Why did you use two stages instead of just one neural network?'
Your Answer:
"Scale and latency. A complex neural network or decision tree takes too much time 
to evaluate against 100,000 items on every page load. By decoupling the system into 
a fast, lightweight vector retrieval stage (high recall) and a sophisticated re-ranking 
stage (high precision), we get the best of both worlds: sub-10ms response time with 
maximum recommendation quality."

Question 2: 'What is CUPED and why does a business care about it?'
Your Answer:
"In online experiments, natural user variation creates huge noise—some people are heavy 
users, others rarely visit. CUPED uses each user's past behavior before the experiment 
began to mathematically subtract out that natural baseline noise. 
This shrinks the variance of our metric by 30% to 50%. For a company, that means 
we need fewer users and can detect whether a feature works weeks earlier, saving 
both server costs and engineering time."

Question 3: 'What is Sample Ratio Mismatch (SRM) and why does it matter?'
Your Answer:
"If you set up an A/B test to split traffic 50/50, but end up with 55% Control and 
45% Treatment, something is broken behind the scenes—maybe the treatment model crashed 
on certain devices or users dropped off due to slow load times. If you ignore this and 
calculate standard stats, your entire experiment conclusion will be completely biased. 
My framework runs an automated Chi-Square test on traffic counts to alert engineers 
immediately if an SRM occurs."
